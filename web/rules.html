Rulebook

Everything the engine actually enforces. Start with the first section and open the rest as you need them — nobody reads a rulebook front to back.

The basicsstart here

Two to six players. Everyone starts on the same square, outside, with a hand of cards: six each with two to four players, four each with five or six.

A turn

On your turn you do one of these:

  • Roll and move. The usual.
  • Trade cards. Stay where you are and hand in two or more cards to draw one fewer than you gave. The card you lose on the exchange is the fee — which is why handing in one card buys nothing and is refused.
  • Nothing. End your turn. Sometimes the best square is the one you are already on.

Before and after the roll there are moments where certain cards can be played — see when cards can be played.

Moving

Roll two dice and move exactly that many squares, one square at a time along the lines on the board. Two rules shape every move:

  • You may not revisit a square in the same turn. Not on a double, not on either half of a split — once you have stood on a square this turn, no later step may re-enter it.
  • Until you are accredited you can only go forwards along the wall walk. No turning round, no cutting into the inner ward.

Where a roll gives you more than one place to end up, you pick from the highlighted squares. Where there is only one, the game takes it for you.

Doubles

A double earns you another turn once this one finishes. Roll a third double in a row and the guards take an interest: straight to the Bloody Tower.

Accreditationgetting off the wall walk

The wall walk is 78 squares of one-way queue, and it dead-ends at Queen's House. Overshooting does not matter: a roll bigger than the distance simply leaves you on Queen's House.

Arriving there puts you on trial. From your next turn, your roll is judged rather than walked:

  • Odd total — accredited. Use that same roll to move freely.
  • A double — the clerks give you another go. (Every double is even, so without this a double would be an automatic failure. The three-doubles rule still applies.)
  • Any other even total — turned away. Your turn ends.

There are two shortcuts:

  • The wall-walk square marked Go to Queen's House (accredited) hands it to you outright, and ends your turn there.
  • A Tower Pass played while standing on Queen's House accredits you without a roll.

Accreditation is permanent. It survives prison, the Rack, and walking out of the Tower and back in again.

The boardfour regions and what they do

The wall walk (78 squares) rings the Tower. One-way until you are accredited, an ordinary two-way path afterwards. Most of the numbered squares that do something live here.

The inner ward (122 squares) is the open middle, reachable only once accredited. Most of it is watched: landing on a watched square turns over a raven card.

The White Tower (24 squares) holds the jewels. It is a single one-way chain — you enter at one end and leave at the other. Inside it, two special rules apply: nothing can move you (no split roll, no Lasso, no card), and nobody can fight. One square past the last jewel is the Rack Sender.

The exterior, south is the strip outside the walls holding the Cradle Tower — the way out — linked back to the inner ward by slides.

Shortcuts

  • Slides link the inner ward to the southern strip. They are ordinary two-way connections — one step each.
  • The secret passage joins the Chapel Royal to the Salt Tower, and costs one step like any other line.
The Shop is a dead end whose only neighbour is the square that sends you to it. So when your missed turn there is served, you are put back out on that square rather than left to walk straight back in.
Stealing a jewelthe whole point

Five jewels sit in a row inside the White Tower. Land on one and you may attempt it.

Roll two dice. You need 12. Every burglary tool you play takes its value off that target — so a Key to the Jewel Cases (worth 9) brings it down to 3, and a couple of Files bring it down to 8.

  • Tools are never used up. Play every one you hold, every time.
  • Failing costs you the turn, not the chance. You stay standing on the jewel and may try again next turn, as often as you like.
  • A double on a theft roll earns an extra turn — but it does not count towards the three-doubles rule. Picking a lock is not charging about the board.

A jewel you are holding is not safe and is not worth anything yet. It can be taken off you in a fight, and it is confiscated if you end up on the Rack. See getting out.

One raven card — the Jewel Glimpse — walks you straight to a named jewel and offers you the attempt on the spot, wherever you were.
Coins, the Cradle Tower, and bankinghow a jewel becomes a win

The coin

The Devereux Tower holds one coin per player. You can hold one at a time, and it is the entire price of using the exit. Coins handed back — by a served Rack sentence, or won in a fight by someone already carrying one — return to the pile for somebody else.

The Cradle Tower

The Cradle Tower is the way out, and it needs a coin. Because reaching the door is the hard part, you do not need an exact roll: if your roll can reach it, it is offered — the same courtesy the wall walk's dead end gets. Walking past it is allowed too; taking the door is always a deliberate choice.

Using the exit costs you everything you are carrying:

  • Your jewels are stashed. They are safe forever and are the only jewels that score.
  • Your coin goes back on the Devereux pile.
  • Your whole hand is shuffled back into the tower deck.

Then you are dealt a fresh opening hand and put back on the start square, still accredited. Leaving is not the end of your game — it is a lap.

Walking out with no jewels is a legitimate play. It is how a player stripped of their hand on the Rack gets a new one.
Fightingtaking it off somebody else

You may start a fight when all of these are true:

  • You end your move on a square an opponent is standing on.
  • You are both accredited.
  • You are holding at least one weapon.
  • You are not in the White Tower. Nobody fights in there.

If your route passes through someone you could fight, you may stop short on them deliberately and take them on instead of moving your full roll.

How a fight resolves

  1. The attacker commits any number of weapons, face-down.
  2. The defender commits any number of weapons, face-down. Only the defender may use the Suit of Armour.
  3. The defender may then play Sanctuary or Mass Accretor (see below).
  4. Both sides reveal. Highest total wins; a tie goes to the defender.

Spoils

  • The winner takes every jewel the loser was carrying.
  • The winner takes the loser's coin. If they already had one, the spare goes back to the Devereux pile.
  • The winner draws replacement cards — one for each weapon they themselves played. Fighting hard is not free, but it is not ruinous either.
  • Every committed weapon is discarded, on both sides.
  • The loser goes to hospital and misses their next turn.

The two defensive cards

Sanctuary calls the fight off. The defender is spirited away to the Chapel Royal, and both players lose the weapons they committed and draw that many replacements — the blows were struck even though the fight did not finish.

Mass Accretor takes one weapon at random out of the attacker's committed pile and adds it to the defender's. It swings twice: the attacker's total drops and the defender's rises by the same amount.

Prison, the Rack, and other misfortuneand how to get out

Missing a turn

The hospital, the two benches and the Shop all cost you your next turn. If somebody drags you off one of those squares before you have served it — a split roll, a Lasso — the debt goes with the square and is written off.

Prison

The Bloody Tower imprisons anyone who lands on it. The Bowyer Tower questions them. The Beauchamp Tower only locks you up via its raven card. All three hold you for three turns, and while held you cannot move and nobody can move you.

Three ways out:

  • Roll a double on your turn — you walk free and use that roll to move.
  • Serve the three turns.
  • Play the right card. A Royal Pardon answers prison or questioning. A Disguise walks you out of the Bloody Tower. A Rope or a Ladder gets you out of the Beauchamp Tower. A Confession, played while being questioned, frames somebody else — you swap places and they serve the rest of your sentence.

The Rack

The Rack is worse than prison in every way. You get there by landing on the Rack Sender in the White Tower, by drawing the Rack of Torment, or by being caught in the White Tower when the Firecrackers go off.

  • It takes all your jewels, and either your coin or your entire hand — the coin if you have one.
  • It holds you for three turns, and rolling does not help. There is no escaping early.
  • You are skipped entirely while you are on it: no roll, no decision, no prompt.

The Rack Pardon is the exception. Play it and the sentence is torn up: your jewels, your coin and your cards all come straight back, and you walk out of the cell immediately. Nothing is actually destroyed until the three turns are served — so the Pardon works right up to the last moment.

The Rack cannot confiscate a Rack Pardon. Taking the one card that answers a Rack sentence, as part of that sentence, would make it a card that could never be played.
Yeoman Warders and Disguiseswalls that move

There are four Yeoman Warder posts and four warders, and at the start of the game every post is manned. A manned post is impassable — you cannot step onto it or through it.

A Disguise gets you past. You do not have to decide in advance: routes through a manned post are offered alongside the free ones and marked, and the card is only spent if you actually commit to one. Stopping short of the post is free.

Raven cards move the warders about — one recalls a warder to the Barracks, several send one back out to a named post. A post already manned cannot take a second warder.

The Disguise has two other uses: it walks you out of the Bloody Tower, and it satisfies a Stopped and Searched card.

Sevens and Binary Disruptionmoving somebody else

Rolling a seven

A seven is not yours alone. You split it between yourself and one opponent, in any division you like — 6 and 1, 4 and 3, or all seven for yourself. You also choose which of you moves first, which matters when one leg's landing changes the other's options.

You can only give steps to someone the roll could actually move. Players who are locked up, or inside the White Tower, are not offered — and if nobody can be moved at all, you simply take all seven.

A leg handed to an opponent is walked in full: you chose its length, so you cannot also stop it short.

Binary Disruption

The card deals out the roll you have just thrown. It is played after the dice land but before you move — you play it because of the numbers you can see.

It rearranges the roll rather than re-adding it, so the two dice go one each. Roll a 5 and a 3 and one player goes 5 while the other goes 3; you cannot split that 8 as 7 and 1, and you cannot keep both. That is what separates it from a natural seven.

If neither die can move anybody, the card stays in your hand rather than being spent for nothing.

When cards can be playedtiming is most of the skill

Weapons and burglary tools are never "played" from your hand — they are committed in a fight or on a theft. Everything else has a window:

The tower deck… cards

The deck you draw from when you land on a tower. Click any card for what it does and how many of it there are. When the draw pile runs out the discards are shuffled and dealt again, so nothing leaves the game for good.

The raven deck… cards

Drawn when you land on a watched square, which is most of the inner ward. The card is dealt face-down and does nothing until you turn it over — so the whole table sees what happened before any piece moves. Only the player who drew it may flip it.

Grouped below by what they do, commonest first. Where several cards share an effect they are listed separately, because the difference between being summoned to the Bell Tower and to the Broad Arrow Tower is the whole card.

Winningtwo modes

Only stashed jewels count. A jewel in your pocket when the game ends never left the Tower and scores nothing. The lobby picks the mode before the game starts.

Fast

The first player to stash a jewel — any jewel — wins on the spot.

Slow

Play runs until one of two things happens:

  • Somebody is beyond catching. With five jewels in play, stashing three ends it: two is all anybody else could still reach.
  • All five jewels have been stashed.

Then the count decides it, in this order:

  1. Most jewels stashed.
  2. The grandest single jewel, if that ties.
  3. Total value, if that ties too.

Jewels rank St Edward's Crown (5), the Prince of Wales's Crown (4), the Sceptre (3), the Orb (2), the Sword (1).

Any player can also end the game early as a draw — the results screen still comes up with everyone's haul.
